# Lecture 0 — The Record Before the Argument

This lecture makes one editorial choice.

Before the case is explained, challenged, protested, reconstructed or interpreted, the reader should hear the record.

The sequence below is chronological.

The quotations retain their source voices.

The point is not that every quotation proves the interpretation later developed in Lectures 1–5.

The point is that the questions those lectures ask are already present in the record before the essays begin.

---

# 29 June 2017 — the GP sends the clinical object

**GP referral to AMU**

> “[Patient] transferred to our practice here in Briarhill in April where I noted him to have quite an unusual festinant and stiff gait — he was attending with bilat foot issues and blamed his gait at that time on callouses and corns (his feet are not in a good state).”

> “Over recent weeks it has become increasingly evident that he is having trouble initiating movements at times and also has marked stiffness and increased tone in both upper and lower limbs with brisk reflexes.”

> “He describes feeling that he cannot relax his muscles and that this is most problematic in bed — that he claws his toes and cannot get comfortable.”

> “My concerns are that there are definite signs of increased tone here with abnormal gait and now affecting his employment and daily activities — ?parkinsonian or other underlying diagnosis but certainly seems to be deteriorating.”

The referral does not contain “Parkinson's plus unrelated feet.”

It contains feet, gait, tone, toe posture, movement initiation and deterioration in one communication.

---

# July 2017 — the hospital compresses the referral

**Hospital discharge synthesis — referral summary**

> “39Y/O WITH DIFFICULTY INITIATING MOVEMENT, INCREASED TONE AND STIFFNESS IN ALL 4 LIMBS, COGWHEELING AT THE WRIST.”

**Hospital history**

> “The onset of symptoms may be 13 years prior i.e. 2004???”

> “[Patient] describes that to ‘be relaxed in bed, [he] would feel that he had tense his body up into certain postures’.”

> “He feels that he first noticed symptoms in the legs and body in 2004.”

> “As he looks back now he feels that the next issue was corns and calluses and the appearance of fallen arches in both feet.”

The long trajectory survives.

But it has already been reorganised.

# July 2017 — Neurology sees an unusual lower limb

**Neurological examination**

> “The gait is strikingly abnormal.”

> “He tends to walk almost with a footdrop appearance somewhat suggestive of a dystonic posture — NOT FOOT DROP.”

> “LLL Kicks out like a foot drop.”

> “Gross bradykinesia.”

> “Pes planus.”

> “Hammertoes.”

**Impression**

> “A Young Onset Parkinsonsm.”

> “? Young onset Parkinson's disease YOPD — ? Syx ongoing x c 13 years to some degree.”

The record does not fail to see dystonic-looking phenomenology.

The later question is how deeply it was phenotyped.

---

# July 2017 — the painful distal territory is given another possible status

**Discharge synthesis**

> “The leg symptoms including corns and callouses and burning may have been completely separate or perhaps a secondary peripheral neuropathy perhaps in somewhat respect related to the significant original alcohol intake? Not clear as this has improved.”

The same lower-limb territory now carries two different evidential roles.

One is neurologically informative.

The other is allowed to become separate, peripheral or uncertain.

# July 2017 — the synthesis becomes more sophisticated

**Genetics section**

> “One of the chief difficulties with this is the question of whether a firm genetic diagnosis will actually actively change management?”

> “This question is (in the majority of cases) usually answered in the negative.”

> “More specifically given a number of issues including his very young age of onset; the akinetic rigidity; the lack of a Dominant family history; and the marked symmetry; as well as how cognitively excellent [Patient] is, there is a question of whether the indicators are that he does have a PRKN gene disorder (PARK 2)?”

> “Accordingly the only real current value of a ‘genetic’ diagnosis is in terms of what it might tell us about the actual neuropathological form of which neurodegenerative condition he has and maybe to some degree with prognostication and an understanding of Levodopa sensitivities?”

Then:

> “In reality, while I am not an expert on these conditions (I am not a movement disorders neurologist) — however I suspect that whatever testing we might send may well be negative and [Patient] has been prepared for this likelihood.”

Then:

> “Additionally there is a question of whether we should just wait and in a number of years do the whole genome or a whole exome analysis when the information underpinning the associations of various genes and various phenotypes has expanded much more from where it is now?”

The document names genotype.

It names phenotype.

It names levodopa sensitivity.

It names an expertise limit.

---

# July 2017 — uncertainty becomes treatment

**Therapy**

> “Whatever form and whatever genetic cause is the cause, the question which is undoubtedly the most important is as regards therapy? Can we improve how the person is?”

> “Ordinarily we would use levodopa or dopamine agonists however in these types of situations we tend to be extremely cautious in their usage for the reasons outlined in the summary on treatment and we will try to leave off on this for as long as possible.”

**Counselling**

> “It is a huge amount to be taken on board — and much more than usual in more ordinary Parkinson's disease presentations.”

> “Moreover, he has a very prescient understanding and will quite appropriately tell us when we are overwhelming with too much information.”

> “[Patient] has a good understanding of why we wished to delay the commencement of levodopa or dopamine agonist type treatments at least for now.”

> “It is possible that he will do very well with levodopa and in a sustainable way whenever it is started.”

> “Indeed in time to come — we may regret that we did not start this earlier.”

Then:

> “Plan/Recommendations:”

> “As per above.”

The reasoning above is now the plan.

# 12 September 2017 — Professor Healy

**Professor Daniel G. Healy to the treating neurologist**

> “Thank you for asking me to see this man who I think probably has Park II and I would suggest testing that initially. If it is negative I would go on to test a panel.”

> “He describes a dramatic improvement in his motor symptoms with Procyclidine low dose qds and Rasagiline 1mg od.”

> “Physical activity would be helpful and as you are aware patients with PARK II tend to manage quite well for long periods of time with relatively low doses of Dopamine replacement therapy. They can become quite dyskinetic if over-used.”

The record now contains a more specific PARK II opinion.

It also contains the phrase “relatively low doses.”

---

# 4 October 2017 — the treatment break produces new information

**Contemporaneous patient email during procyclidine withdrawal**

Before withdrawal:

> “My walk is back together.”

> “My writing is good.”

> “I can stand up straight.”

> “My legs are loose and limber.”

Four days into the break:

> “I am feeling the tension building up, my feet are restless and my walk is falling apart....but my eyesight is back to normal.”

The medication change alters the body.

The feet move with the neurological state.

# 2021 — levodopa finally enters

**Neurology clinic**

> “[Patient] had felt a bit undertreated.”

> “[Patient] was given what would be viewed in fairness as a very tiny dose of Sinemet.”

> “That was 62.5 mg twice daily (12.5/50mg BD) as the first start of a Levodopa drug.”

Later in the same note:

> “I do not want the Sinemet rising significantly above where we are.”

> “It is a general principle of Parkinson's management all over the world that we try and spare Sinemet for as long as possible in people who are very young.”

> “Additionally when Sinemet is used, the aim should be to keep the dosing as absolutely minimal as possible.”

The risk of treatment remains highly visible.

# March 2025 — the treatment relationship becomes impossible to ignore

**Treating neurologist's complaint response**

> “In essence we have known that Mr Moloney has Parkinsons; we have known it would be Sinemet or other dopamine agonist responsive; we have tried to spare commencing those for some time as is common practice in the management of younger people with Parkinsons.”

> “it has been known and acknowledged that Sinemet helps his experience of morning pain”

> “his Lyrica pregabalin treatment introduced for the neuropathic pain (of whatever cause) in part to try to spare further Sinemet increase”

> “now his Sinemet treatment has been recently escalated at clinic; with a plan from clinic to do this slowly and carefully as Sinemet CR; which has provided him with profound relief currently.”

Then the same response describes the purpose of the record itself:

> “the primary interest is to be a resource for the person themselves, who has to be at the heart of everything we do.”

> “They are also written so as to try to clearly bridge the understandable gap between ‘what is said or intended versus and what is heard/understood’.”

The record is not only a handoff instrument.

It is explicitly described as part of the patient's understanding.

# September 2025 — institutional validation

**SIMT outcome**

> “the multidisciplinary membership of the SIMT were satisfied that decisions made in relation to care at Galway University Hospitals were appropriate.”

> “The SIMT were satisfied that risk factors considered when determining the appropriateness of starting Sinemet at such an early age, given the risk of associated side effects.”

> “Alternative potential diagnoses were considered and Sinemet was first prescribed in 2021, therefore it was determined that there was no deficit in the care provided.”

The treatment-risk logic of the historical record has now entered the governance conclusion.

---

# February 2026 — the later clinical conclusion

**Consultant neurology genetic-results letter**

> “They confirm that he has two pathogenic variants in the Parkin gene as predicted.”

> “His Parkinsonism and dystonia are due to compound heterozygous mutations in the Parkin gene.”

The later answer cannot be projected backwards as knowledge available in 2017.

But it identifies the disease that had been present while the earlier record was being made.

---

# 2026 — the genetic report describes the phenotype

**Diagnostic genetic report**

> “Indication: Early-onset Parkinson disease; dystonia, onset in 20s.”

> “Detection of two pathogenic variants in gene PRKN, which are causative for juvenile Parkinson disease type 2 in your patient if present in compound heterozygous state.”

And in its phenotype description:

> “Characteristic features are bradykinesia, resting tremors, rigidity and lower-limb dystonia, the latter of which sometimes remain an isolated finding for years.”

> “The disease is slowly progressive and shows a good response to L-DOPA treatment.”

Again: this is 2026 evidence.

It does not tell us what 2017 clinicians were required to know.

It tells us what biological disease was ultimately identified.

# The reading rule for Lectures 1–5

The essays that follow are not substitutes for these quotations.

They are attempts to explain their relationships.

Whenever an essay becomes more confident than the record permits, return here.

Whenever an argument becomes abstract, return here.

Whenever the patient, clinician or institution is assigned knowledge, ask which quotation establishes it.

The record comes first.

The essays have to earn everything else.
